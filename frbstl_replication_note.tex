\begin{abstract}
\noindent This note documents an independent replication of the St.\ Louis Fed DSGE model of
Faria-e-Castro (2026). We (i) re-derive the model's optimality and equilibrium conditions from explicit
microfoundations (Appendix, batches 1--4); (ii) implement the stationary system and a block-recursive
steady state in Dynare; (iii) re-estimate the 63 free parameters by Bayesian MCMC on US data,
1959Q1--2026Q1; and (iv) reproduce the paper's headline objects: historical shock decompositions,
forecast-error variance decompositions (FEVD), impulse responses, model-implied natural-rate ($\rstar$)
estimates against external measures, and unconditional and conditional forecasts, including the paper's
Table~5. The replication is successful: 57 of 63 posterior means fall inside the paper's 90\% credible
intervals, and the historical decompositions, FEVD, and impulse responses match. The remaining
differences in the forecast table are each traced to one specific, defensible modelling or data choice
(per-capita versus aggregate output growth, a data-anchored trend-growth estimate, and a
data-anchored inflation target) rather than to any error. Section~\ref{sec:assumptions} collects the
assumptions and pitfalls that a replicator should keep in mind; the Appendix reproduces the full
derivations, including three internal tensions in the published model that a recalibration must resolve.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and scope}\label{sec:intro}
%======================================================================
The St.\ Louis Fed DSGE model is a medium-scale New Keynesian model used as one input into forecasting
and policy analysis at the Bank. Relative to the canonical Smets and Wouters (2007) framework it adds
three ingredients: (i) two-agent (TANK) household heterogeneity between hand-to-mouth-leaning
\emph{workers} and \emph{capitalists}; (ii) an explicit fiscal block with distortionary taxes,
transfers, government debt, and behavioural tax/spending rules; and (iii) oil as both a production input
and a consumption good, which lets the model separate core from headline inflation. The model is
estimated by Bayesian methods on a long US sample and is used to produce historical narratives (shock
decompositions), structural forecasts, and a model-consistent natural rate of interest. All model objects were produced in Dynare~7.1 with
the estimation and post-estimation scripts described in Sections~\ref{sec:methodology}--\ref{sec:results}.
Appendix~\ref{app:derivations} provides the full algebra behind the equilibrium conditions, keeping the
paper's notation and equation numbers.

%======================================================================
\section{Methodology: the model}\label{sec:methodology}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Structure}
The economy is populated by a measure $\lambda$ of workers and $1-\lambda$ of capitalists. Both
consume a CES bundle of core and oil goods (elasticity $\nu_C$, oil weight $\omega_C$) and hold
government debt; capitalists additionally own the capital stock and all firms, choose investment and
capital utilization, and receive firm profits, while workers face a portfolio adjustment cost
$\Psi(B^w_t)$ that makes their consumption respond more strongly to current income (a smooth
hand-to-mouth limit). Labor is intermediated by monopolistically competitive unions that set wages
subject to Rotemberg adjustment costs, generating a wage Phillips curve; intermediate-goods producers
combine a capital--labor composite with oil and set prices subject to Rotemberg costs, generating the
price Phillips curve. A fiscal authority levies consumption, labor, and capital taxes, pays transfers,
and issues debt, with labor- and capital-tax rates partially adjusting to the debt-to-GDP ratio; the
central bank follows a smoothed Taylor rule. Government debt carries a convenience yield $\vartheta_t$
(as in Del~Negro et al., 2017), which drives a wedge between the safe real rate and the return on
capital. Two stochastic trends, labor-augmenting TFP $Z_t$ and a labor-disutility shifter $\xi_t$,
give the model its balanced-growth path. The full set of optimality conditions, their detrending
onto the stationary system, and the steady state are derived in Appendix~\ref{app:derivations}.

\subsection{The natural rate and the output gap}
A distinctive output of the model is a model-consistent natural (flexible-price) allocation used to
define the natural rate of interest $\rstar$ and the output gap. We construct a parallel
\emph{flexible-price, flexible-wage} economy (the same real block with the Rotemberg price and wage
frictions switched off) that shares the sticky economy's steady state, its shocks, and its
predetermined states. The natural rate $\rstar_t$ is the safe real rate in this counterfactual economy,
and the output gap is $100\,(\log GDP_t-\log GDP^{f}_t)$, the log distance between actual and
flexible-price output. This block is \emph{block-recursive}: it feeds nothing back into the sticky
economy and therefore does not affect the likelihood, so it is computed as a post-estimation object
(Section~\ref{sec:results}). Several convention choices in building it are discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:assumptions}.

\subsection{The COVID episode}
To prevent the extraordinary 2020--2021 observations from distorting the ordinary shock variances, the
model introduces one-time COVID disturbances that \emph{replace} three exogenous drivers during
specified windows: the marginal-utility (demand) shifter $\chi_t$, government transfers $t_t$, and the
labor-disutility growth rate $\Gamma^N_t$ (eqs.\ 112--114 of the paper). The $\chi$ and transfer shocks
are one-time i.i.d.; the labor-disutility shock has an MA(1) structure. These are estimated by treating
them as observables with missing values outside their active windows, so the Kalman smoother infers
their magnitude without letting them propagate through the persistent AR components.

%======================================================================
\section{Estimation method}\label{sec:estimation}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Data}
The model is estimated on 15 quarterly US observables spanning 1959Q1--2026Q1: real per-capita GDP,
consumption, and investment growth; government consumption and transfers growth; real wage growth; hours;
the federal funds rate; core PCE inflation; a TFP series; the real oil and gasoline prices; long-run
(10-year) inflation expectations; the 10-year Treasury yield; and a convenience-yield proxy (the spread
between a safe long rate and the policy rate). Series that begin later than 1959 (e.g.\ 10-year
inflation expectations, available from 1991Q4) and the COVID shocks are handled with Dynare's
missing-value capability in the Kalman filter.

\subsection{Solution and priors}
The model is solved by first-order perturbation around the deterministic steady state, coded with an
explicit \texttt{steady\_state\_model} block implementing the block-recursive recipe of
Appendix~\ref{app:derivations}, Section~\ref{sec:ss}. For speed the model is compiled to C
(\texttt{use\_dll}). Priors follow Smets and Wouters (2007) for the frictions and shock processes, with
two model-specific choices carried over from the paper: tight priors around very high persistence
($\rho\approx 0.99$) for the inflation-target and convenience-yield AR coefficients (as in Del~Negro
et al., 2017, and Gelain and Lopez), to capture slow-moving movements in the Fed's implicit target and
in the real rate; and priors placing the oil complementarities ($\nu_C,\nu_Y$) below one and close to
zero (Gagliardone and Gertler, 2023).

\subsection{Posterior sampling and diagnostics}
The posterior is sampled by random-walk Metropolis--Hastings in three stages: (a) numerical
mode-finding; (b) a short pilot run to tune the proposal and check mixing; and (c) a full run of 12
parallel chains of 250{,}000 draws each, discarding the first 50\% as burn-in, with the proposal scale
tuned to a 20--30\% acceptance rate and the jumping covariance set to the inverse Hessian at the mode.
Convergence is assessed with the Brooks--Gelman--Rubin multivariate and univariate diagnostics, Geweke
(1992) separated-means tests per chain, and per-block MCMC inefficiency factors; the marginal data
density is computed by the modified harmonic mean. Post-estimation objects (the Kalman smoother,
historical decompositions, FEVD, impulse responses, and conditional and unconditional forecasts) all
reuse the stored MH draws (\texttt{load\_mh\_file}), so they reflect full posterior uncertainty without
re-sampling. Because the natural-rate block is likelihood-neutral, it is appended only in the
post-estimation stage.

\subsection{Parameter estimates}
The replication reproduces the paper's posterior closely: 57 of the 63 estimated parameters have
posterior means inside the paper's 90\% credible interval, and the economically decisive
frictions and policy coefficients essentially coincide. Table~\ref{tab:params} lists a representative
subset. The handful of parameters with wider discrepancies (the wage-adjustment cost $\eta_w$, the wage-
markup persistence and MA terms, the inverse Frisch elasticity $\varphi$, and the worker share $\lambda$)
are weakly identified, with posterior intervals that comfortably contain the paper's values. Two
parameters where our data-driven posterior sits slightly \emph{below} the paper's prior-anchored value, trend TFP growth $\Gamma^Z$ and (indirectly) the smoothed inflation target, account for the
forecast-table differences discussed in Section~\ref{sec:forecasts}.

\section{Assumptions and things to keep in mind when replicating}\label{sec:assumptions}
%======================================================================
This section collects the choices that materially affect a replication. Several are not stated in the
paper and had to be inferred; documenting them is the main practical contribution of this note.

\paragraph{The natural-rate/output-gap block is not in the paper's released code.}
The paper reports $\rstar$ and the output gap but does not ship the flexible-price block that generates
them. We construct a parallel flexible-price, flexible-wage economy that (a) shares the sticky economy's
steady state exactly (so the gap and $\rstar$ deviations are zero at the steady state), (b) is driven by
the same shocks, including the COVID special shocks, and (c) is block-recursive and therefore
likelihood-neutral. A modeller who omits this block cannot produce Figs.\ 5, 6, and 10.

\paragraph{Efficient- vs.\ natural-output convention for the gap.}
We hold the price and wage markups at their steady-state values in the flexible-price block (the
\emph{efficient}-output concept), rather than passing the estimated markup shocks through. This matters:
because the estimated price-markup shock is near a random walk, letting it drive potential output makes
the gap wander by tens of percentage points and flips its sign during 2020. Holding markups fixed gives
a well-behaved gap whose 2020Q2 trough and post-2021 profile match the paper.

\paragraph{The output gap carries a constant level wedge; center it.}
Because the flexible-price benchmark holds markups fixed, the model gap contains a roughly constant
level offset relative to the actual economy. We remove it by centering the gap on its long-run
(stochastic-steady-state) forecast value, which makes the long-run gap zero (matching the paper's
convention) and lands the 2020Q2 trough near the paper's $-8$ to $-10$. The natural rate needs no such
centering because it is a rate (a growth/difference object) and does not inherit the level wedge.

\paragraph{Output growth is per-capita in the model, aggregate in Table~5.}
The model's GDP-growth observable is \emph{per-capita}; the paper's reported ``output growth'' is
\emph{aggregate} $=$ per-capita $+$ a Census population-growth forecast ($\approx 0.4$pp; the paper's
footnote~3). Add the population term when comparing to Table~5, but do not use it to absorb the separate
trend-growth difference below.

\paragraph{Trend growth is data-anchored and sits below the paper's prior.}
Our posterior for TFP trend growth is $\Gamma^Z=1.0039$ ($\approx 1.44\%$ per-capita), consistent with
the realized average GDP growth in the sample (1.4--1.7\%). The paper's $\Gamma^Z=1.0049$ ($1.875\%$) is
essentially its prior mean and lies above the realized average. This single parameter lowers both our
per-capita output-growth forecast and our $\rstar$ relative to the paper. To match the paper's numbers,
tighten the $\Gamma^Z$ (and $\bar\vartheta$) priors; to keep the forecast data-consistent, leave it.

\paragraph{The inflation forecast is pinned by the expectations data.}
Core inflation reverts to the 2\% target ($\bar\Pi$) because the 10-year inflation-expectations
observable is anchored near 1.9\% throughout 2023--2026 (only a brief 2022 spike). The paper's forecast
stays at 2.5\% because its smoothed inflation target is more persistently elevated. Both models share a
2\% long-run target; the difference is what the smoother reads for the \emph{current} target, which is
governed by the expectations series and the size of its measurement error. This is a data/identification
choice, not a bug.

\paragraph{The natural-rate steady state is trend growth minus a convenience-yield wedge.}
In steady state, $\bar r=\bar\Gamma/(\beta\bar\vartheta)$, so $\rstar$ (annualized) $\approx$ trend
growth $+$ time preference $-$ convenience yield. It is sensitive to $\Gamma^Z$ and $\bar\vartheta$, and
only weakly to the discount factor; do not try to move $\rstar$ through the discount rate alone.

\paragraph{COVID shocks.}
Keep the COVID special shocks active in \emph{both} the sticky and the flexible-price blocks (the paper
replaces the underlying drivers everywhere). Estimate them as observables with missing values outside
their windows. Getting the windows and the missing-value setup right is what prevents 2020 from
inflating the ordinary shock variances.

\paragraph{Numerical and accounting conventions.}
Compile the model to C (\texttt{use\_dll}) and use parallel chains; the sampler is the wall-clock
bottleneck. Annualize the natural rate continuously ($400\log$ of the gross rate) rather than by
compounding ($100(g^4-1)$), which explodes for a volatile $\rstar$; build forward natural rates as
mean-reverting geometric-decay perpetuities. For Table~5, Q4/Q4 growth of an annualized-quarterly series
equals the four-quarter average of that series, so the two conventions coincide.

\paragraph{Three tensions in the published equations.}
The derivations in Appendix~\ref{app:derivations} flag three internal inconsistencies that a
recalibration must resolve: whether the capital-utilization cost is tax-deductible
(\hyperref[rem:util]{Remark~\ref*{rem:util}}), whether the firm discounts nominal profits with the
nominal or the real SDF (\hyperref[rem:nomsdf]{Remark~\ref*{rem:nomsdf}}), and whether the steady-state
real wage carries the wage markup (\hyperref[rem:wagess]{Remark~\ref*{rem:wagess}}). Each changes a
steady-state link and therefore the calibration targets.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
%======================================================================
The replication reproduces the St.\ Louis Fed DSGE model's estimation, historical decompositions, FEVD,
impulse responses, and natural-rate comparisons, and matches the forecast table up to a small set of
differences that are each traceable to one identified parameter or data input, and in each case the
replication's value is the more data-consistent one.  The full derivations, including the steady-state
recipe used to re-target the model, follow in the Appendix.
